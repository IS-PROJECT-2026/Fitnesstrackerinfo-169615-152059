An independent explainer

Your tracker is a very good estimate machine. It is not a medical instrument.

Roughly one in four adults now wears something that counts their steps, watches their heartbeat and grades their sleep. Almost none of them are told how the measurement actually happens — or where it quietly breaks down. This page explains both.

01

How they work

A tracker has no idea what you are doing. It has a handful of sensors producing raw signal, and a pile of statistics guessing at the rest. Knowing which is which tells you how far to trust any given number.

Optical heart sensor

Measures: pulse rate, heart rate variability

LEDs shine light into the wrist and a photodiode measures what returns. Blood volume changes with every beat, so the reflected light flickers in time with your pulse. This is the sensor doing most of the work on any modern device.

Accelerometer

Measures: motion, steps, sleep proxy

A tiny mass on springs, etched into silicon, registering acceleration on three axes. Steps are not counted directly — the device pattern-matches the wobble of your arm against what walking usually looks like.

Gyroscope

Measures: orientation, rotation

Tells the device which way the wrist is turned and how fast it is rotating. Combined with the accelerometer, it separates a swimming stroke from a bicep curl from a steering wheel.

GPS receiver

Measures: position, pace, distance, elevation

Trilaterates your location from satellite timing signals. Genuinely measured rather than inferred, which is why outdoor pace is one of the more trustworthy figures a tracker gives you. It is also the fastest way to drain the battery.

SpO₂ sensor

Measures: estimated blood oxygen

Red and infrared light this time, because oxygenated and deoxygenated blood absorb the two differently. The ratio gives an estimate. On consumer wearables this is a wellness feature, not a clinical one.

Electrical heart sensor

Measures: single-lead ECG rhythm

You complete a circuit by touching the case, and the device records the heart's electrical activity. Some are regulatory-cleared to flag irregular rhythms. A flag is a prompt to see a doctor, not a diagnosis.

Skin temperature

Measures: deviation from your baseline

Reports how far tonight sits from your own normal, rather than an absolute reading. Useful for spotting the onset of illness or tracking a menstrual cycle. Room temperature and bedding influence it more than most people expect.

Bioimpedance

Measures: tissue resistance

A small current is passed through tissue and the resistance measured. Marketed for body composition and hydration. Hydration, food and time of day move the reading enough that single measurements mean little.

The part worth remembering: only GPS position, electrical rhythm and raw acceleration are genuinely measured. Steps, calories, sleep stages, stress and recovery scores are all derived — the output of a proprietary model that each manufacturer keeps to itself and revises without telling you.

02

What is on the market

The category has split into five recognisable shapes. Models change every year; the shapes have been stable for a while, and picking the right shape matters more than picking the right model.

Fitness bands

Fitbit Charge · Samsung Galaxy Fit · Amazfit Band

The original form. A small screen, a strap, most of the sensors, several days of battery. The cheapest honest way into tracking.

  • + Light, affordable, long battery
  • − Cramped screen, limited apps

Smartwatches

Apple Watch · Samsung Galaxy Watch · Pixel Watch

A wrist computer that also tracks fitness. The widest sensor array and the most regulatory-cleared health features, at the cost of charging most nights.

  • + Most features, best app ecosystem
  • − Priciest, shortest battery, locked to a phone platform

Sports watches

Garmin Forerunner · Coros · Polar · Suunto

Built for training rather than notifications. Multi-band GPS, structured workouts, route navigation, and battery measured in weeks rather than days.

  • + Best GPS accuracy and training analysis
  • − Bulky, and the data can overwhelm a beginner

Smart rings

Oura · Samsung Galaxy Ring · Ultrahuman

Sensors moved to the finger, where the signal is cleaner and the device is easy to forget you are wearing. Strongest at sleep and recovery.

  • + Comfortable overnight, discreet, good signal quality
  • − No screen, no GPS, sizing is permanent, often a subscription

Screenless straps

Whoop · Fitbit Air · Amazfit Helio

Deliberately no display, so there is nothing to check mid-session. Everything lives in the phone app, usually behind a monthly fee.

  • + No screen anxiety, comfortable, recovery-focused
  • − Subscription dependent — stop paying and the data stops

Note Specific models move fast. Verify current availability and pricing before citing any device by name.

03

What they are genuinely good for

The honest case for wearing one.

Making the invisible visible

Most people badly misjudge how much they move. A tracker replaces a guess with a record, and that alone shifts behaviour for a while.

Trends beat readings

Any single number is noisy. Watched over weeks, the same sensor becomes genuinely informative — a resting heart rate drifting down over a training block is real signal.

Catching the outlier

Irregular rhythm notifications have sent people to a cardiologist who had no symptoms at all. Rare, but it is the clearest documented benefit of the whole category.

Structure for training

If you are training for something specific, pace, heart rate zones and route data turn vague effort into a plan you can follow and review.

Feedback on sleep timing

Stage detection is shaky, but bedtime and wake time are measured reasonably well — and consistency of timing is the part that matters most.

A shared record with a clinician

Months of resting heart rate or activity data can be a useful conversation starter in a consultation, provided it is treated as context rather than evidence.

05

Risks and limitations

The part the marketing leaves out. None of these mean you should not own a tracker — they mean you should own one knowingly.

Where your health data actually goes

Heart rate, location and sleep records are unusually revealing. Consumer wearable data generally sits outside medical privacy law, governed instead by a privacy policy the company can revise. Read what is shared with third parties, and check whether the company has been acquired since you signed up.

False reassurance

A normal-looking reading is not clearance. Trackers miss things they were never built to detect, and a good score has talked people out of getting real symptoms checked. Symptoms outrank scores, always.

False alarm

The reverse problem. Consumer devices generate a meaningful rate of readings that look alarming and turn out to be nothing, which produces anxiety and unnecessary appointments.

Orthosomnia

A documented pattern where anxiety about improving a sleep score makes sleep worse. People start optimising for the number their device reports rather than for how rested they feel. If checking the score is the first thing you do each morning, that is worth noticing.

Compulsive tracking

For some people constant self-quantification stops being motivating and starts being intrusive — exercising through injury to protect a streak, or feeling unable to eat or rest without logging it. If tracking has begun to feel compulsory rather than useful, that is a reason to talk to someone rather than to change devices.

Motivation that fades

Abandonment rates are high, often within the first six months. The device is a measuring tool, not a source of motivation, and it cannot supply a reason to exercise that you did not already have.

Subscriptions and lock-in

Several brands put the analysis behind a monthly fee, and some hardware is worthless without it. Export options are often limited, so years of history can be difficult to take with you when you switch.

Cost and lifespan

Sealed batteries degrade in a few years and are rarely replaceable, so these are effectively disposable computers. Factor the replacement cycle, and the electronic waste, into what the device really costs.

06

Choosing and using one well

Questions that matter more than the spec sheet.

  1. Name the one question you want answered

    "Am I moving enough?" needs a cheap band. "Why am I always tired?" points to a ring. "How do I get faster over 10k?" needs a sports watch. Buying before you know the question is how a drawer fills with abandoned hardware.

  2. Check the phone it has to live with

    Some ecosystems are locked to one platform, and several features disappear or degrade on the other. Confirm before you buy, not after.

  3. Add up the subscription, not just the price

    A cheaper device with a mandatory monthly fee can cost more across three years than an expensive one without. Work out the three-year figure.

  4. Find out how you get your data out

    Before committing years of history to a platform, check that a full export exists and what format it comes in.

  5. Wear it properly

    Snug, a finger's width above the wrist bone, tighter during exercise. Most complaints about heart rate accuracy are strap problems.

  6. Read trends, ignore single days

    Compare this week to last month, not today to yesterday. One bad night is noise. A four-week drift is information.

  7. Take a day off from it

    If leaving it on the charger for a day feels genuinely uncomfortable, the relationship has inverted — and that is worth paying attention to.
